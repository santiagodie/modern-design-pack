## 1. Problem / Pain Section

**Becomes mandatory when:** traffic is cold, the category is new, or the visitor doesn't yet realise they have the problem.

### Job
Make the visitor feel *understood*. Show you know their situation better than they could describe it — then pivot to the solution.

### Psychology
- **Loss aversion:** people are more motivated to avoid a loss than to achieve an equivalent gain. Describing the cost of the status quo (time, money, stress, risk) creates motivation.
- **The "mirror" effect:** when copy uses the exact words a person uses to describe their own problem, they assume the solution was built for them.
- **PAS framework (Problem → Agitate → Solve):** name the problem, make its consequences concrete, then introduce the product as the way out.

### Content rules
- 2–4 pain points, maximum. Each is concrete and recognisable ("Chasing 14 people for receipts every month-end"), not abstract ("Inefficient processes").
- Quantify the cost if possible ("Teams lose ~6 hours a week to…") — but only with a real source.
- Use **Voice of Customer (VoC)** language: copy phrases from reviews of competitor products, support tickets, sales calls, forum threads.
- End with a **pivot line** to the solution ("There's a better way.") leading into the benefits section.
- Tone: empathy, not fear-mongering. You're the guide, not the alarm.

### Layout options
- **Before/after split:** "Without [product]" vs "With [product]" columns (clear, scannable; very effective).
- **Pain list:** 3 short statements with small illustrations or crossed-out icons.
- **Narrative paragraph:** a short story of a typical day (works for B2C and services).
- **Cost calculator teaser:** "How much is manual invoicing costing you?" (links to a calculator — see `03_NICE_TO_HAVE.md`).

### Sizes & spacing
- Short section: 64–120 px vertical padding. This is a bridge, not a chapter.
- If using before/after: equal column widths, the "after" side visually warmer/brighter (color, check icons).

### 🔴 Critical errors
- Exaggerated or manipulative fear ("Your business will fail without…"). It triggers skepticism and, in regulated sectors, legal issues.
- Insulting the visitor's current choice ("If you're still using spreadsheets, you're doing it wrong").

### 🟠 Common errors
- Too long: three paragraphs of misery before any hope.
- Generic pains every competitor lists.
- Describing the problem in the company's words, not the customer's.

### 🟢 Level-up fixes
- Lift a real quote from a customer interview describing the pain, with their name and role.
- Use a single striking stat with a source link.
- Make the visual contrast between "before" and "after" do the work (e.g. a messy spreadsheet vs. a clean dashboard).

### 💎 Insider notes
- The best problem sections are written **after** 5–10 customer interviews. Ask: "What were you doing right before you started looking for a solution?" The answer is your problem section.
- For hot traffic (people who already searched for your solution), **cut this section** or reduce it to a single line. Explaining a problem to someone who already knows it slows them down.

## 2. How It Works

**Becomes mandatory when:** the product is unfamiliar, the process is non-obvious (services, marketplaces, hardware setup, financial products), or the perceived effort is a barrier.

### Job
Reduce **perceived effort and uncertainty**. Show the path from "now" to "result" is short and clear.

### Psychology
- **Perceived effort:** people overestimate how hard new things are. Showing 3 simple steps lowers the mental "cost".
- **Goal-gradient effect:** a visible path with few steps makes the end feel reachable.
- **Rule of three:** three items feel complete and are easy to remember.

### Content rules
- **3 steps** (4 maximum). Each step: number + verb-led title (2–5 words) + one sentence.
- Step 1 must sound **effortless** ("Connect your bank — 2 minutes"). This is where the reader decides whether it's "for them".
- The last step is the **outcome**, not an action ("Get paid 2× faster").
- Add **time estimates** where possible; they convert vague effort into a concrete, small number.

### Layout options
- **Horizontal numbered steps** with a connecting line (desktop), stacking vertically on mobile.
- **Vertical timeline** with a small visual per step.
- **Sticky scrollytelling:** product visual pinned, steps scroll alongside and update the visual.

### Sizes & spacing
- Step numbers: large (32–64 px) and in the accent or a muted brand color — they're anchors for scanning.
- Step titles 20–24 px; descriptions 16–18 px; max ~25 words each.
- Gap between steps 32–64 px; connector lines 1–2 px.

### 🔴 Critical errors
- Steps that reveal hidden effort ("Step 3: Schedule onboarding call with our team (2 weeks)") without framing it as a benefit.
- Mismatch between steps here and what actually happens after clicking the CTA.

### 🟠 Common errors
- 6–8 steps (feels like work).
- Steps that are features in disguise rather than a sequence.
- Generic icons per step instead of real visuals.

### 🟢 Level-up fixes
- Show a **real screenshot** for each step.
- Place a CTA right after the last step: the reader has just been shown how easy it is — strike while that's fresh.

### 💎 Insider notes
- If the honest process is long (enterprise onboarding, construction, legal), *don't hide it*. Group it into 3 phases and say "We handle steps 2–5 for you." Removing effort beats hiding effort.

## 3. Visual Demonstration

**Becomes mandatory when:** you sell software, a physical product, or any experience that's hard to imagine.

### Job
Let the visitor **see** the product doing the job. "Show, don't tell" is the most reliable persuasion rule in product marketing.

### Psychology
- **Concreteness:** people trust what they can see. A screenshot of a real result beats a paragraph describing it.
- **Mental simulation:** seeing the product used helps visitors imagine themselves using it, which increases purchase intent.
- **Aesthetic-usability effect:** a clean, polished UI shown in its best state creates the expectation that the product is easy.

### Format options (by effort and impact)
| Format | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Annotated static screenshot | Every SaaS | Cheapest, fastest to load, very effective |
| Short looping video/GIF (5–15 s) | Showing a flow or "magic moment" | Muted, no controls needed if short; offer pause |
| Explainer video (60–120 s) | Complex or new categories | Play button with a poster frame; show duration |
| Interactive demo (clickable product tour) | PLG SaaS, complex tools | High lift; see `03_NICE_TO_HAVE.md` |
| 3D/AR product viewer | Physical products | Heavy; lazy load and offer static fallback |

### Content rules
- Show the product in a **realistic, populated state** (realistic data, real names, no lorem ipsum).
- **Crop tight** on the relevant part; zoom in. A whole dashboard at 30% scale is unreadable noise.
- **Annotate**: arrows, highlights or short labels pointing to what matters.
- Videos: put the "aha moment" in the **first 5 seconds**, include captions (most people watch muted), state the duration on the play button ("Watch the 90-second demo").

### Sizes & spacing
- Product visuals usually deserve **the full content width** (1100–1280 px) or more (bleeding off the edge creates a sense of scale).
- Soft, low-contrast shadows and a subtle 1 px border separate UI screenshots from the background better than device mockups.
- Video aspect ratio fixed in CSS (`aspect-ratio: 16/9`) to avoid layout shift.

### 🔴 Critical errors
- Autoplaying video with sound.
- Embedding a YouTube/Vimeo iframe directly in the first viewport (adds hundreds of KB of JavaScript before the user even clicks; hurts LCP and INP).
- Screenshots of an old UI version (customers notice and feel misled after signing up).

### 🟠 Common errors
- Dated device mockups (glossy iPhones and iMacs) around the UI — they add weight and look templated.
- Video longer than 2–3 minutes on a landing page.
- No poster image, so the video area is a black box while loading.
- Illustrations of "people using a laptop" instead of the product.

### 🟢 Level-up fixes
- Use a **lightweight video facade**: show a static image with a play button; load the real player only on click.
- For autoplay loops, export as **MP4/WebM** (not GIF; GIFs are often 5–10× larger).
- Recreate screenshots as **simplified, high-fidelity UI** (remove irrelevant clutter, enlarge key text). Many top SaaS sites show "idealised" versions of their UI — acceptable as long as everything shown exists.

### 💎 Insider notes
- Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`: swap autoplay loops for static frames for users who've asked for less motion.
- The best "demo" for a B2B page is often a **2-minute founder-narrated screen recording**, slightly imperfect. It reads as authentic, and authenticity is a scarce signal in a world of AI-polished marketing.

## 4. Testimonials & Case Studies

**Becomes mandatory when:** the offer is high-ticket, B2B, a service, or from a little-known brand.

### Job
Provide **specific, believable evidence** from people like the visitor that the promised outcome is real.

### Psychology
- **Social proof** works through *similarity*: a testimonial from someone in the visitor's role, industry and company size is far more persuasive than a famous name from a different world.
- **Specificity = credibility:** numbers, timeframes and concrete before/after details make a testimonial believable; vague praise reads as fake.
- **Imperfection signals honesty:** mixed-tone quotes ("Setup took a while, but…") and ratings below 5.0 are perceived as more credible.
- **Placement:** proof works best next to the objection it answers. Lovable cites a VWO case where Buildium increased free-trial sign-ups by 22% just by improving the *headings* on existing homepage testimonials — framing and placement, not new content.

### Content rules
Every testimonial needs:
- **Full name** (initials read as fabricated or evasive)
- **Photo** (real, not stock)
- **Role and company** (+ company logo for B2B)
- **A specific outcome** (number, timeframe, before/after)
- Ideally a **bolded "headline"** extracted from the quote (the 5–10 words that matter)

Case studies on a landing page should answer, in order: *who the customer is → what problem they had → what they did → what result they got (numbers)*. Link to the full story.

**How to get better testimonials:** don't ask "Can you write a testimonial?" Ask: "What was it like before? What changed? What would you tell someone in your position?" Then edit for length (with their approval).

### Layout options
- **Featured testimonial:** one large quote with a big photo — best placed right after the benefits.
- **Wall of love:** a masonry grid of many short quotes (tweets, reviews) — signals volume.
- **Case study cards:** logo + key metric (huge number) + one line + "Read story".
- **Contextual testimonials:** single quotes placed next to the feature or objection they address.
- **Video testimonials:** highest credibility; use a facade/poster, captions, 30–90 s.

### Sizes & spacing
- Featured quote: 24–36 px text, line-height 1.3–1.4, max 2–4 lines. Quote marks can be oversized decorative glyphs.
- Card quotes: 16–18 px; card padding 24–32 px.
- Metric callouts: 40–72 px numbers.

### 🔴 Critical errors
- Fabricated testimonials, AI-generated faces, or stock photos labelled as customers (illegal in the EU and US; catastrophic if discovered).
- Anonymous quotes ("— J., Marketing Manager").

### 🟠 Common errors
- **Testimonial carousels** that auto-rotate: most visitors see one slide; the rest are hidden content.
- Generic praise ("Great product, amazing support!").
- All testimonials in one block at the bottom instead of near the objections they address.
- Quotes that are too long (people read the first line and skip).

### 🟢 Level-up fixes
- Add a **bold pull-headline** to each testimonial.
- Match testimonials to segments (show a testimonial from an agency on the "for agencies" landing page).
- Combine on-site quotes with **third-party verification** (G2, Trustpilot, Google Reviews widgets or links).
- Show the **metric in large type** next to the quote ("−67% month-end time").

### 💎 Insider notes
- Refresh testimonials regularly; dates and old UI screenshots in reviews signal an abandoned product.
- A single endorsement from a respected expert in a niche can outweigh dozens of anonymous reviews — authority + similarity combined.
- If you're early-stage with no customers: use **beta-tester quotes**, a founder's credibility story, or a public build log. Never fake it.

## 5. Pricing

**Becomes mandatory when:** the product is self-serve or the price is a primary decision factor. Hiding pricing behind "Contact sales" is a deliberate choice that costs conversions from smaller buyers.

### Job
Make the decision **easy**, frame the value, and guide visitors to the plan that's right for them (and for you).

### Psychology
- **Anchoring:** the first price seen sets the reference. Showing a high-value tier makes the target tier look reasonable.
- **Center-stage effect:** in a set of three, buyers gravitate to the middle option as the "safe" choice. Research attributed to Price Intelligently suggests three-tier pages convert roughly 1.4× better than two-tier pages, with four or more tiers performing worse (directional data).
- **Decoy effect:** a slightly inferior option makes the target option look better. Kinde warns that a decoy must still be a *viable* option — an obviously bad deal makes visitors feel manipulated.
- **Choice overload (Hick's law):** too many tiers or comparison rows freeze decisions.
- **Pain of paying:** monthly framing, "per user" framing and annual savings expressed as "2 months free" reduce felt cost.

### Content rules
- **3 tiers** is the default (+ optional "Enterprise / Contact us" as a 4th lightweight column or row).
- Each tier: name (who it's for: "Starter", "Team", "Business"), price, billing period, **one-line description of the ideal customer**, the CTA, and the 4–6 most important inclusions.
- Highlight one tier ("Most popular" / "Recommended") — usually the middle one.
- Annual/monthly toggle: default to the option you prefer (often annual) and show the saving ("Save 20%" or "2 months free").
- Under the cards: a **full comparison table** (collapsible) for detail-seekers.
- Directly under or beside pricing: **pricing FAQ** (billing, cancellation, refunds, taxes, what happens at the limit).
- **EU B2C:** prices shown to consumers must include VAT. B2B pages may show "excl. VAT" but should say so explicitly.

### Layout, sizes & spacing
- Cards: equal width, 280–360 px each; 16–32 px gap. The highlighted card can be slightly taller or elevated, with an accent border or background.
- Price: 40–56 px, with the currency smaller and the billing period ("/month") much smaller and muted.
- Inclusions: check icons, 15–16 px text, 8–12 px spacing between items.
- On mobile, stack cards with the **recommended plan first** (or use tabs/segmented control to switch plans).

### 🔴 Critical errors
- Hidden fees revealed at checkout (setup fees, mandatory add-ons). Destroys trust and can breach consumer law.
- Prices without currency or billing period.
- Comparison tables with 60 rows and no grouping.
- A "most popular" badge on a plan that isn't actually the most popular (false claim; risky in the EU).

### 🟠 Common errors
- Five or more tiers on the main view.
- Identical-looking cards so nothing guides the eye.
- Feature lists in internal jargon ("API quota tier 2").
- CTA copy identical across tiers with no context ("Get started" ×3).
- No answer to "What happens when I exceed limits?".

### 🟢 Level-up fixes
- Tailor CTA copy per tier ("Start free", "Start 14-day trial", "Talk to sales").
- Show **"best for"** labels under each tier name so visitors self-select by identity, not by feature math.
- Show per-user price *and* an example total for a typical team size.
- Place a guarantee or "Cancel anytime" line directly under the pricing cards.
- Add a usage-based pricing **slider/calculator** if price depends on volume.

### 💎 Insider notes
- Price endings signal positioning: **charm pricing** ($49) reads as "value"; **round pricing** ($50, $500) reads as "premium". Don't mix both in one table.
- Ordering tiers **high-to-low** (most expensive first) strengthens anchoring; low-to-high feels friendlier. Test for your audience; most SaaS uses low-to-high with the enterprise tier as an anchor.
- The anchor must be a **real, purchasable plan**; an absurd anchor gets dismissed and stops working.
- Express annual discounts as "2 months free" — concrete time feels more valuable than an abstract percentage for many audiences.
- A pricing page is often the most-visited page by visitors about to buy. Sales teams use pricing-page visits as a strong intent signal — so track them as an event.

## 6. Trust Elements

**Becomes mandatory when:** you request payment, personal data, health/financial information, or ask a B2B buyer to put their reputation on the line.

### Job
Remove the **fear of risk** at the moment of commitment: risk of losing money, data, time, or face.

### Psychology
- **Risk reversal:** a guarantee shifts risk from buyer to seller, which removes the biggest barrier for hesitant buyers.
- **Authority:** recognised certifications, media logos and expert endorsements borrow credibility.
- **Local perception of security:** Baymard's research shows users judge security *locally* — some parts of the same page feel more secure than others. Security signals must sit **next to** the sensitive input (payment fields, forms), not in a far-away footer.

### Trust signal inventory
| Signal | Where it belongs |
|---|---|
| Money-back guarantee / free trial terms | Under the pricing cards and the final CTA |
| "Cancel anytime" / "No contract" | Under the CTA microcopy |
| Security & compliance badges (SOC 2, ISO 27001, GDPR, HIPAA) | Near forms and in a security section for B2B |
| Payment method logos & "Secure checkout" | Next to payment fields |
| Press logos ("As seen in") | Near the hero or in the proof strip |
| Awards & certifications | In the proof strip or near the final CTA |
| Real contact info (address, phone, team photos) | Footer, about block; strong for services and B2B |
| Uptime/status, response time | Near CTAs for B2B/technical buyers |
| Data handling statement | Under the form ("We'll never share your data") |

### Layout, sizes & spacing
- Badges: small, monochrome or muted (they're reassurance, not the main message); 24–40 px tall.
- Guarantee: a recognisable "seal" or a highlighted box with an icon, short title and 1–2 lines.
- Keep the signal **within the same visual group** as the action it reassures (8–16 px from the button or form).

### 🔴 Critical errors
- Fake badges ("100% Secure" generic seals, "Award-winning" without an award). Savvy visitors recognise them, and in the EU they can be misleading commercial practice.
- Guarantees with hidden conditions that make them unusable.

### 🟠 Common errors
- All trust elements clustered in the footer, far from forms and payment.
- Too many badges (looks like a cheap site).
- Certifications listed without explanation for non-technical buyers ("SOC 2 Type II" → add "Independently audited security").

### 🟢 Level-up fixes
- Translate certifications into benefits for the buyer ("Your data is stored in the EU").
- For B2B, add a dedicated **Security / Trust Center** link with documentation (security reviews are often a gating step in the deal).
- Use a **named guarantee** with personality ("The 30-day no-questions-asked promise") — memorable and confident.

### 💎 Insider notes
- Strong guarantees often *reduce* refund rates relative to the conversion they add, because they attract confident buyers and signal quality. Test before assuming they'll cost you.
- Trust has two layers: **credibility** (is this a real, established company? → logos, numbers, press, early on the page) and **security** (is it safe to give them my data/money? → badges and statements at the form/checkout).

## 7. FAQ

### Job
Handle the **remaining objections** that would otherwise stop a ready visitor, and pre-empt support questions.

### Psychology
- Each unanswered question is a reason to postpone. "I'll check later" usually means "never".
- **Serial position:** put the biggest objection first and a confidence-building answer last.
- Visitors who open FAQ items are often close to buying — they're running final checks.

### Content rules
- **5–10 questions**, chosen from real sales calls, support tickets, chat logs and on-site search — not from what the marketing team *wants* to say.
- Typical objection categories: price & billing, cancellation & refunds, setup effort & time, compatibility/integrations, data & security, support, "Is this right for my case?".
- Write questions **in the visitor's voice** ("Can I cancel anytime?"), and answers that **start with a direct yes/no** followed by the detail.
- Keep answers to 2–4 sentences; link out for depth.
- No "fluff" questions ("Why is [product] the best?").

### Layout, sizes & spacing
- **Accordion** is the standard: use native `<details>`/`<summary>` or an accessible button with `aria-expanded`.
- Question text 17–20 px, weight 500–600; answer 16–17 px, max-width ~65ch.
- Each row: 16–24 px vertical padding; full row clickable; a clear +/− or chevron indicator.
- Two-column layout on desktop is fine for long lists (left: section title and a contact link; right: accordion).
- Place **after pricing**, before the final CTA.

### 🔴 Critical errors
- Answers that dodge the question (visitors notice and assume the worst).
- FAQ content that contradicts pricing or terms elsewhere on the page.

### 🟠 Common errors
- Only one item open at a time with heavy animations (annoying when comparing answers).
- 30 questions (becomes a help center).
- Icons or chevrons that are tiny and not keyboard accessible.

### 🟢 Level-up fixes
- Add a "Still have questions? Chat with us / Book a 15-minute call" line under the FAQ.
- Bold the key phrase in each answer for scanners.
- Make the FAQ searchable if it's long (rare on a landing page, useful on a pricing page).

### 💎 Insider notes
- `FAQPage` structured data is still valid markup and helps machines (including AI search engines) parse Q&A content, but since 2023 Google shows FAQ rich results mainly for authoritative government and health sites. Don't add an FAQ *for* SEO snippets; add it for the humans and for AI answer engines.
- Your FAQ is a **copy research tool**: if one question keeps getting the most opens (track accordion clicks), that objection belongs higher up on the page, probably near the hero CTA.
